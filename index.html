Three Games, Three Approaches

Three games.
One evolving genre.

Defined by the games that pioneered it—Konami's Castlevania and Nintendo's Metroid—the Metroidvania genre focuses on 2D exploration, uncovering secrets, and gathering abilities to discover even more. But where is the genre now? Here, I break down three of the most recent additions to the genre: Hollow Knight: Silksong (2025), Well Dweller (2026), and Fallen Tear: The Ascension (2026).

Spoilers for all three games ahead!
01 / THE REVIEW

Hollow Knight: Silksong

I'll start with Hollow Knight: Silksong, the recent addition by Team Cherry. This game is a nigh-perfect representation of the genre. In fact, it's the game I'll be using as a baseline for comparison with the other two.

Silksong is a direct sequel to Team Cherry's previous game, Hollow Knight, taking place in a new kingdom with the beloved character Hornet. The game has you explore the remnants of the kingdom of Pharloom and the society that lives around it.

What Makes It Great

Silksong takes the usual formula of gaining abilities and turns it up to the max. Not only do you gain new traversal abilities, but you also gain new tools and crests that change how you fight.

Pharloom is also twice as large as Hallownest, the location of the previous game, and it feels filled with secrets to explore and discover. There's probably no bigger secret than the entirety of Act Three, which you may not even realize exists until you discover it.

Where It Falls Short

The biggest point of friction in this game is its difficulty. While most Metroidvanias are known for being challenging, Silksong takes it to a new level: every boss feels like a struggle, as each hit can take a devastating two points of health.

Where Hollow Knight's tutorial was King's Pass, the tutorial for Silksong is Hollow Knight itself.

This difficulty makes the game less approachable and can leave players frustrated. For example, when fighting the Last Judge, widely regarded as one of the hardest Act One bosses, I easily died thirty times before I finally "got good."

02 / THE REVIEW

Well Dweller

Well Dweller puts you in the shoes—or should I say talons—of a bird on a quest to rescue his family and save them from being made into a cloak for the evil queen.

The game is filled with diverse areas, each with its own story and tools to help you on your adventure.

What Makes It Great

Well Dweller approaches the question of vertical movement in a unique and exciting way. Where most games give you a double jump, Well Dweller instead has you take your matchstick and use it as a springboard to reach greater heights.

Where It Falls Short

Well Dweller's greatest problem can best be described as what I call the "Zelda Problem."

This is best explained by an item from The Legend of Zelda: Twilight Princess: the Spinner. It's an amazing item, and it feels endlessly fun to use. The problem is that it's next to useless outside of its designated dungeon.

Well Dweller commits this same sin with its region-specific abilities: the Flight Feather and Wind Glide. While both abilities are fun and engaging, they're only applicable to their respective regions. Worst of all, they aren't even utilized in their regions' boss fights, where they could have added an additional layer of depth.

Additionally, the postgame content is exceptionally weak. It adds a new ability that only ever needs to be used four times to reach small areas that don't offer any additional content beyond a few small rooms.

03 / THE REVIEW

Fallen Tear: The Ascension

Fallen Tear has you play as Hira, who gathers allies and explores forgotten corners of the world, all in an effort to save the world from corruption while discovering the secrets of his past.

What Makes It Great

While most Metroidvanias involve exploring an otherwise hostile world, Fallen Tear: The Ascension has you gathering allies through its unique Fated Bonds system. This system gives you constant companions who react to the environment around you.

Additionally, the game has solid art direction, smoothly blending its environments with the enemies that inhabit them.

Where It Falls Short

At the beginning of the game, Fallen Tear bombards you with tutorials. With so many items to collect, the game is riddled with pop-ups that disrupt your gameplay at the very point where you should be most eager to explore.

Traversal ability progression is tied to the Fated Bonds system, which involves acquiring allies. By collecting enough bonds, you can unlock abilities such as a double jump.

This system is flawed because regions in the game are locked behind these traversal abilities, yet the game fails to properly guide players toward finding the bonds they need, often leaving them feeling stuck.

Another problem is ability bloat. The game has a total of 22 magical abilities tied to your allies. With so many choices, the system can easily feel daunting.

Additionally, the system gets in its own way through the team attack mechanic. This mechanic charges up as you fight, and once it's fully charged, your allies perform a powerful combo attack.

This ability quickly became the only way I used my allies in combat, leaving me without much reason to explore the true depth of the system. The ability became my default instead of feeling like something truly special.

04 / LOOKING AHEAD

Metroidvanias Now and Into the Future

Metroidvanias will always have a special place with their emphasis on exploration and discovering the secrets around you.

Silksong is a story of Team Cherry taking its tried-and-tested formula, expanding on it, and rocking the world in the process. Well Dweller takes classic problems and offers new answers not seen elsewhere. And finally, Fallen Tear is a story of how new and exciting mechanics don't always add the depth players crave.

As I eagerly await the upcoming DLC for Silksong, I challenge you to look for the secrets in both the fantasy worlds you experience and the world around you. Consider unique solutions to old problems and, arguably most importantly, don't forget about the people around you who can help you get further than you ever could alone.
